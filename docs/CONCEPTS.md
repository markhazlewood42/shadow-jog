# Game-dev and JRPG concepts, learned along the way

Mark asked (2026-09-30) for a running record of the game-dev and JRPG ideas that come up while building Shadow Jog, so the project doubles as a way to learn the craft. Each entry says what the idea is in plain words, then where it shows up in this game. `docs/GLOSSARY.md` is for the game's own names (places, people, items); this file is for the craft.

**Keeping it current:** whenever a new concept comes up in the work or in conversation, it goes in here, in the same change. Add the date it came up if it's useful context.

---

## Pixel art

*The craft in depth, in teaching order with sources and Shadow Jog notes: `knowledge/pixel-art/README.md`.*

- **Pixel density (resolution).** How many screen pixels one art pixel covers. Mixing densities (a chunky character next to a finely drawn monster) reads as a flaw, because the eye sees two different pixel sizes on one screen. *Here:* the field is drawn 1:1 at 480×270; the battle world was 240×135 drawn at 2×, so human enemies were chunky while creatures (`res 2`) were fine. The PixelLab art pass makes everything at the field's density. *Side battle:* the traced enemies are about 2x2 blocks (56 to 67 per cent of cells are one colour) and the crew about 1 art pixel per screen pixel, so side-view enemies are collapsed to the crew's density first (`collapseBlocks` in `src/art/rig2/enemy.ts`).
- **Palette and colour count.** Pixel art usually limits itself to a small set of colours; a limited palette makes a set of sprites look like they belong together. *Here:* prompts ask for "at most 15 colors"; the art-pass "Today's colours" options take the palette from the game's own screens.
- **Outline.** A dark line around a sprite so it reads against any background. Styles: a solid black outline, a single-colour outline, or *selective* outlining (darker shades of the sprite's own colours on the lit side). *Here:* "bold black outline" is part of the house recipe.
- **Shading tones.** How many steps of shadow a surface gets (flat, one tone, two tones). More tones look richer but noisier at small sizes.
- **Dithering.** Mixing two colours in a checkerboard or ordered pattern to fake a third, or to blend one area into another. *Here:* terrain edges blend with an ordered (Bayer 4×4) dither in `src/field/tiles.ts`.
- **Chibi proportions.** Characters with big heads and short bodies (about 2–3 heads tall). Standard for small overworld sprites, since the face stays readable at 28 px. *Here:* field sprites are chibi; battle sprites are about 5 heads tall.
- **Anchor (ground point).** The pixel of a sprite that sits on the ground, so characters of different heights stand on the same line. *Here:* `CharSprite.ax/ay`.
- **Scale2x (EPX).** An old upscaling trick that doubles pixel art while keeping diagonals smooth instead of blocky. *Here:* the old battle sprites were drawn small and scaled with it (`src/art/pix.ts`).
- **Style reference (style image).** Giving an AI tool an existing image to copy the palette, outline and shading from, so new art matches old. *Here:* the art pass's most reliable lever; describing the look in words worked better than naming a game.
- **Procedural art.** Art generated by code rather than drawn. *Here:* everything in the game until the art pass: letter-grid characters, painted terrain, synthesised music.
- **Palette discipline and colour merging.** Pixel art uses a small set of colours so shading stays deliberate; an AI generator gives a thousand near-identical shades instead. Merging shades that are almost the same into the commonest one (by colour distance, most frequent first) restores a palette without redrawing, and doing it across all of one character's frames keeps a shade from drifting between poses. *Here:* `cleanColours` in `rig2/sfcrew.ts` on Mark's Sprite Fusion sprites: 5,830 shades became 143, with no visible change. Round 4: the merge distance is per character (`clean` in `SfSpec`): Kit and Hex at 24, Sable at 20, Rook at 16 (his steel arm needs its shading). At 24 Kit falls from 146 colours to about 70 with no visible loss at 5x; at 32 her jeans go black, which is the limit.
- **Scaling pixel art by 1.5 (scale3x and a vote).** Doubling is easy (every pixel becomes 2x2); 1.5 normally gives some pixels one size and some another, which looks uneven. Scale3x triples the sprite while keeping diagonal edges as diagonals, then each 2x2 block votes for one pixel, so the result stays on a clean grid. *Here:* tried on the enemies in the Sprite Fusion layout (1.5x and 1.75x) and judged worse than not scaling: the grain still differs from the crew's and faces go mushy. Round 3 uses whole multiples only (see pixel density, below).
- **Pixel density (texel density).** One screen pixel per art pixel is the crew's grain; a sprite drawn at 2x2 blocks next to it looks like a different game, however good the art is. Scaling by a whole number keeps a sprite's grain even; by 1.5 it does not. The honest fix for a size gap is art made at the right size, not a scaler. *Here:* enemies are 1x native (small beside the crew) and bosses 2x (the backdrop's grain); new enemy art at crew density is on Mark's Sprite Fusion list.
- **Depth lines and the ground plane.** In a side-on battle the party stands on a diagonal: members further back stand higher on screen, because the ground recedes. Sprites do not shrink with distance in this style, so a steep diagonal makes the back ones look like they float on the scenery, and a shallow one reads as a single line. The backdrop's floor decides how far back anyone may stand. *Here:* `SF_SLOTS`, feet 78 to 60, held up only by the command menu; the sewer backdrop (a corridor seen end-on) has no floor for it.
- **Packing sprites by pixels, not boxes.** Two sprites' bounding boxes can overlap while their pixels never touch (a raised sword over a head). Spacing a line-up by pixel masks (every frame of one against every frame of the next, with a pixel or three of clearance) packs it tighter than boxes allow and keeps it safe. *Here:* `tests/sflayout.test.ts`.
- **Dash versus walk cycle.** A walk cycle needs several drawn frames per step; with one pose you can still sell speed: move fast, add a bounce and trailing ghosts, and skid into the stance. It reads as a dash and only works over a short run; anything slower on one pose reads as sliding. *Here:* Kit's and Rook's entrance in the Sprite Fusion battle; Hex and Sable fade in over a short step because they have no run frame.

## Sprites and animation

- **Sprite sheet.** One image holding every frame of a character, laid out in a grid, so the game draws one rectangle of it at a time.
- **Directions (rotations).** Top-down games need a character from each side they can face: 4 directions (down, right, up, left) or 8 with the diagonals. *Here:* the game uses 4; PixelLab makes 8.
- **Walk cycle.** The loop of frames for walking. Classic JRPGs use 3–4 frames: stand, left foot, stand, right foot. *Here:* the old sprites use stand/step/stand/step; PixelLab's are 4 drawn frames (`CharSprite.walk`).
- **Camera views.** *Top-down* looks straight down; *low top-down* (the classic JRPG "¾ view") sees the front and top of things; *high top-down* is closer to straight down; *side view* is a platformer's. PixelLab asks which one.
- **Back-view battle.** Phantasy Star IV (and Shadow Jog) show the party from behind at the bottom of the screen facing the enemies, rather than side-on (Final Fantasy) or first-person (Dragon Quest).
- **Skeleton / template animation.** Animating a character by fitting it to a stick-figure skeleton and moving the skeleton through a stock motion (walk, punch, cast), instead of drawing each frame. Cheap and consistent, less expressive. *Here:* PixelLab's template animations (1 generation per direction).
- **Key poses (limited animation).** Instead of a smooth cycle, a move is 2–3 held drawings (wind-up, strike, recover) with code doing the timing, movement and shake between them. Cheap to draw and easy to keep consistent. Phantasy Star IV's battles work this way: a caster is three frames from behind (standing, arm rising, arm up with a spark). *Here:* the plan for the drawn battle sprites after PixelLab's full animations failed Mark's review (2026-09-30).
- **Smear frames and motion arcs.** Drawing the motion itself into a frame: a stretched limb, or a white arc where the sword swept. One frame then reads as fast movement. PSIV draws the slash arc into its attack frames. *Here:* the battle renderer already trails tinted "smear" copies behind a strike; arcs and sparks per pose are drawn in code.
- **RotSprite.** The standard way to rotate pixel art without it turning to noise: scale the sprite up 8x with
  Scale2x (which keeps edges clean), rotate that, then sample it back down. *Here:* rig v2 swings legs about the hip
  with it (`src/art/rig2/rig.ts`).
- **Side-view battle.** The party on one side, the enemies on the other, everyone seen from the side, as in Final Fantasy and RPG Maker's side-view battlers (64 px cells, a fixed list of poses: wait, swing, thrust, cast, damage, dying...). The party usually stands in a shallow diagonal so nobody hides behind anybody. *Here:* the `spike/side-battle` spike tests it against the back view (`docs/spikes/side-battle.md`).
- **Cutting a limb out of a sprite.** To pose one arm separately you have to lift it off the body. When the sleeve is the same colour as the torso (Rook's coat), cutting by colour fails, so you cut by shape: every pixel within a few pixels of the arm's bone. Then the torso behind it is filled in once, and the arm is turned (RotSprite) or replaced by an arm drawn in code. *Here:* `src/art/rig2/side.ts`.
- **Shrinking pixel art.** Averaging neighbouring pixels (what a photo editor does) blends colours and gives muddy in-between shades; snapping them back to the palette helps but leaves noise. For art that was already pixel art, picking one pixel per block (nearest) keeps the palette and the crisp edges. *Here:* rounds 1 to 3 shrank the traced 99 px views to about 46 px by nearest, which is below their native size; round 4 collapses them to native instead (see *Native resolution* below).
- **Scale mismatch.** Characters and enemies drawn at different sizes in one scene read as different games: a 30 px hero next to a 91 px enemy looks like an extra. Battle sprites are often drawn one size up from the field sprites for this reason. *Here:* field Rook is 30 px, the Rustfang Punk 91 px, and the spike compares a 46 px battle size.
- **Wait loop (ping-pong, 1-2-3-2).** A character standing still isn't one frame: it breathes. RPG Maker's side-view "wait" motion has 3 frames played 1-2-3-2, so the loop goes forward then back and never snaps from the last frame to the first. *Here:* the battle-scale crew stretch their chest by a row or two (the head rises, the feet stay) in three frames, each member started at a different point so they don't breathe in step (`src/art/rig2/sidecrew.ts`).
- **Row stretching for breathing.** The cheapest idle animation for small pixel art: draw one row of the torso twice, so everything above it moves up a pixel while everything below stays put. No new colours, no repainting. *Here:* `rise()` in `sideops.ts`.
- **Walking in as a formation.** At the start of a fight the party steps in from the screen edge. If each member walks from the same edge at the same speed to their own place, the ones with the shortest trip stop and the rest catch up and overlap them. Moving everyone the same distance keeps the formation's spacing the whole way. *Here:* `sideWalk()` in `battle.ts`.
- **Size hierarchy (bosses large, regulars party-sized).** In side-view JRPGs the party and ordinary humanoid enemies are about the same height; creatures and bosses are drawn much bigger to look threatening (Final Fantasy VI's bosses fill half the screen). A 91 px punk next to a 47 px hero reads as a giant, not a thug. *Here:* the spike's default (`fit`, round 3) draws humanoids at the trace's native size (a punk 46 px, the crew's height) and creatures at exactly twice it (a Glowrat about 47 px wide); `&enemyscale=half` makes creatures native too, `&enemyscale=wide` is round 2's 1.25x/1.5x look, `&enemyscale=full` is the old 91 px; bosses are never reduced.
- **Integer scaling.** Enlarging pixel art by a whole number (2x, 3x) keeps every pixel the same size. Enlarging by a fraction (1.25x) has to double some rows and columns and not others, so lines come out uneven and outlines look beaded. If a sprite is too small, use 2x or redraw it; don't stretch by a fraction. *Here:* round 2's 1.25x enemies were the cause of the "muddy" complaint; round 3 uses 1x for humanoids and 2x for creatures.
- **Layer resolution.** A game often draws different things onto canvases of different sizes: here the battle world is 240x135 (each pixel shown 2x2) while the enemies get a screen-resolution layer. Drawing a 47 px sprite onto the small layer samples it 2:1 and throws away three pixels in four, which looks like noise however good the art is. A sprite must be drawn onto a layer whose pixels match its own. *Here:* round 2's crew sat on the 240x135 layer and looked broken; round 3 draws them on the enemy layer (`render.ts`), at one art pixel per screen pixel.
- **Colour grading by lightness floor.** To stop a dark sprite vanishing on a dark background, raise the lightness of its darkest colours but keep their hue, and ease the saturation down in proportion, or a near-black navy comes out as bright blue. Do it on the palette (a dozen colours), not on pixels. *Here:* `grade()` in `src/art/rig2/sidecrew.ts`; trousers and boots get a higher floor because they stand on the darkest ground.
- **Two-tone rim light.** A cool light edge on the back and top of a figure, a warm one on the front edge, matches a neon street that lights from both sides; the enemies already carry a warm rim, so the crew shares the same light. A part only one pixel thick (a staff) gets no rim, or it turns into a pale line.
- **Active-actor outline.** Besides an arrow, the character whose turn it is gets a one-pixel outline in the turn colour, so a small sprite reads as "the one choosing" without a big marker.
- **Ground line per backdrop.** The rows where feet should stand differ by backdrop (a street's pavement, a sewer's walkway). Keep it as a small table of offsets rather than nudging individual enemies. *Here:* `SIDE_ENEMY_LIFT_BY_BG` in `src/scenes/battlekit/sideview.ts`.
- **Collapsing 2x2 blocks (not nearest).** To bring block-drawn art down to its real resolution, try each of the four ways the grid of blocks can start and keep the one where most blocks are a single colour; then give each block its commonest colour and drop pixels that touch nothing. Taking one pixel in four (nearest) breaks 1-block-wide lines, like a rim light, into stair steps and leaves stray dots.
- **Rim light.** A thin bright edge on the side of a figure that faces a light, or on its back edge against a dark backdrop. It separates a dark character from a dark street. *Here:* the crew get a cool rim on their back and top edges, and a lifted outline instead of near-black (`finish()` in `sidecrew.ts`); the enemies already carry a warm one.
- **Contact shadow.** A soft dark oval under a character's feet so it sits on the ground instead of floating; it stays on the ground when the character jumps or lunges. *Here:* every crew member and enemy has one in the side battle.
- **RPG Maker's diagonal.** Party slots go down and to the right (or left) one step at a time, so the first panel's character is nearest the enemies and the line of sprites reads in the same order as the status panels below. *Here:* `sideSlot()` in `src/scenes/battlekit/sideview.ts`.
- **Safe zones for battle windows.** Menus and info boxes cover whatever is under them, so a battle layout reserves areas for them: here the left column (command menu) and the open ground under the enemies' feet (ability list, target info), with nothing standing there. When the party flips to the right side, the windows have to be re-planned, not just the sprites.
- **Native resolution of block-drawn art.** AI-traced "pixel art" is often drawn in 2x2 blocks, so its real (native) resolution is half its pixel size. Shrinking it by some other factor (0.41) samples unevenly and loses detail; collapsing the blocks to native is exact and gives the sprite at the size an artist would have drawn it, one screen pixel per art pixel. *Here:* the crew come out 49 to 59 px tall, which is also the "hero scale" the spike wanted (`collapseBlocks` in `src/art/rig2/enemy.ts`, used by `sidecrew.ts`).
- **Three-quarter view for faces.** In small pixel art a pure profile shows a sliver of face; a three-quarter view (turned toward the viewer) shows both eyes, a nose and the front of the costume, which keeps characters recognisable. *Here:* every crew member uses the `south-west` view, facing left and a little at the camera.
- **Solid leg lift (row removal).** To lift a foot in a small walk cycle without rotating it (rotation leaves ghost pixels), take two rows out of the shin and shift what is below up: the leg shortens, stays one solid shape and the outline stays continuous. *Here:* `bendLeg` in `src/art/rig2/sideops.ts`.
- **Lunge, anticipation and follow-through.** A strike that only plays an effect on the target feels weightless; the attacker draws back (anticipation), dashes (a speed smear helps the eye), holds the blow while the hit lands (with the hitstop), then returns. Phantasy Star IV and Chrono Trigger both step the attacker to the target. *Here:* `sideBeat` in `sideview.ts` and `reachX/reachY` in `playback.ts`.
- **Timeline keyed to the hit, not to the clock.** When a move's blow has to land on a moving target time (here, the frame the effect starts on, which is later while a timing ring is closing), the attacker's frames are laid out backwards from that frame: the cut frames and the contact are fixed to it, and any spare time goes into the guard and the held wind-up, never into the blow. The wind-up can then wait for the ring without the blade arriving early. *Here:* `kataTimeline(at)` in `rig2/sidekata.ts`; playback tells the body `at` through `dd.strikeAt`.
- **Fixed bones, movable grip.** A limb keeps one length in every frame (or it visibly grows between frames). When a pose asks for a hand the arm cannot reach, the target is pulled in until it can, rather than the arm stretching. For a two-handed grip both arms must reach their fist, so the whole grip is pulled in. *Here:* `KATA_BONE` and `within()` in `rig2/sidekatadraw.ts`.
- **Kendo form as animation reference (chudan, furikaburi, men-uchi, zanshin).** A real sword strike has a readable shape: a guard with the point at the throat (chudan), the sword lifted overhead (furikaburi), a cut that ends with the arms extended and the front foot stamping (men-uchi), then staying ready (zanshin, "remaining mind"). Each is a key pose a viewer can name, which is why it animates well in only seven frames. *Here:* `KATA_POSES` in `rig2/sidekata.ts`.
- **Draw and sheathe (nukitsuke, noto).** A sword that is on the character's back in one frame and in their hands in the next "pops". Kendo and iaido treat the draw and the return as part of the form: the hand goes to the hilt, the steel clears the scabbard, the blade sweeps up into the guard; and the return is the same in reverse. In animation that is two extra key poses, and the return can reuse the draw keys read backwards. *Here:* `draw1` and `draw2` in `rig2/sidekata.ts`, played at the start and (reversed) at the end of Rook's strike.
- **Weapon first, flash second (a lead frame).** A hit effect is bright, so if it starts on the same frame the weapon arrives it hides the very contact it is celebrating. Show the weapon meeting the target a few frames early and start the flash after it, so the eye sees the steel touch before the light. *Here:* `KATA_LEAD` (3 pose frames) in `rig2/sidekata.ts`, and the effect is anchored a little toward Rook (`KATA_EFFECT_SHIFT`) so the blade's point stays beside it.
- **Far arm behind the body (side-view layering).** In a side view only the near arm crosses the torso; the far arm is behind it, so only its hand shows past the coat or above the head. Drawing both arms in front is what turns a raised sword into a pale wedge across the face. *Here:* Rook's coat-sleeve arm is drawn under the body in `sidekatadraw.ts`, with just its fist on top.
- **Attack lane and depth cue.** An attacker that must reach a target past other enemies cannot always go round them in a flat 2.5D scene. Common answers: run on a row in front of the line (so the dash reads as in front, never behind an ally), and fade whoever the body is covering so the blade stays readable. *Here:* `KATA_LANE_*` in `playback.ts` picks the row; `dimOver` in `render.ts` halves an enemy's or crewmate's opacity while Rook's body is on it.
- **Afterimages that fill the gaps.** A dash that moves 15 px between two drawn frames looks like teleporting; trailing copies of the body at its recent positions (one and two frames back, fading) fill the gap and read as speed. The copies should be the plain body, not the smear arc, or the arc is drawn three times. *Here:* `drawKataFx` in `render.ts` draws the previous two beats' frames, tinted like the slash.
- **A dust puff in stages.** A stamping foot kicks up dust: three short stages (small and dark, round and pale, wide and thin) tied to the frame the foot lands read as weight, where a handful of loose pixels reads as noise. *Here:* the puff under Rook's front foot at contact, in `drawKataFx`.
- **Hit flash on the sprite's own pixels.** A hurt flash should recolour the character's silhouette (white for the first frames, then a faint tint) rather than wash a blob over it, so the shape and outline survive; a knockback of a couple of pixels that springs back sells the weight. *Here:* the side view's hurt in `render.ts`.
- **Tracing (rotoscoping, loosely).** Taking finished art as the base and rebuilding the animation around it in
  your own system. *Here:* rig v2 traces the PixelLab standing frames Mark liked, then animates them in code, so they
  can't drift between frames the way PixelLab's own animations did (2026-09-30).
- **Colour quantization.** Reducing an image to a small palette by merging near-identical colours. It cleans up
  noisy shading and makes recolouring possible. *Here:* each traced character keeps 28 colours shared across its
  four facings.
- **Inpainting.** Asking an image tool to redraw only a masked area and keep everything else exactly as it was ("the arm, raised overhead"). For sprite work it keeps a character's identity while changing a pose.
- **Cutout (skeletal) animation.** A character cut into rigid pieces (upper arm, forearm, hand) hung on a skeleton of joints; a pose is just angles, so a limb turns at its joints and can't stretch, and new poses cost nothing to draw. *Here:* the plan for the battle backs after rig v2's drawn forearm stretched (2026-09-30).
- **Foreshortening.** Something pointing toward or away from the viewer looks shorter than it is: a fist punched straight at the camera is mostly fist, hardly any arm. Sprite artists draw it by hand; a 2D rig has to fake it (shrink the bone, swap the drawing). *Here:* the open question behind Kit's strike reaching *forward* into the screen (Mark, 2026-10-01).
- **Projection.** How a point in 3D lands on the 2D screen. *Orthographic* drops the depth (things don't shrink with distance); *perspective* shrinks what's far. A JRPG battle seen from behind and a little above uses a tilt: a step toward the enemy also moves a point up the screen. *Here:* the 2.5D poses (`project` in `src/art/rig2/battle.ts`: half a pixel up per pixel of depth, and a slight shrink for the hand).
- **Depth sorting (z-order from depth).** Giving each part a distance from the camera and drawing the far ones first, so an arm passes in front of or behind the body as it moves. The basis of "2.5D" rigs: 2D pictures, a little 3D bookkeeping.
- **Attachment swaps.** In 2D skeletal tools (Spine, DragonBones), a bone can show a different drawing depending on the pose: an open hand, a fist, a fist seen end-on. It's how those tools fake turns and depth with flat art.
- **Pre-rendered sprites.** Modelling and animating a character in 3D, then rendering each frame to a small 2D sprite. Donkey Kong Country (1994) and StarCraft were made this way; Dead Cells renders 3D models to low-resolution pixel art without smoothing.
- **Inverse kinematics (IK).** Posing a limb by where its end should go: drag the hand, and the elbow angle that gets it there is worked out. Two-bone IK (shoulder, elbow, hand) has a neat closed-form answer. *Here:* how the animation editor will let Mark pose an arm.
- **Counterbalance (the free arm).** A body swinging something heavy throws its other arm the opposite way to stay balanced: a swordsman's free hand goes out and back as the blade comes across, a pitcher's glove arm pulls in as the ball arm whips. Animators use it to make a pose read as weight and effort instead of one arm moving on a statue. *Here:* Rook's other arm goes out to the side when he has one hand on the sword (his Cast and Victory poses), set once in the editor (pink dot; Mark, 2026-10-01); his two-handed cut puts both hands on the hilt instead.
- **Wind-up and cut (kendo).** Kendo's basic cut has two halves: *furikaburi*, the sword raised over the head, the blade dropping behind the back; and *furiotoshi*, the snap down through the target with both hands, the left at the pommel driving it and the right by the guard steering. A sprite sells it with the blade going behind on the way up and a big arc in front on the way down; Chaz's slash in Phantasy Star IV is six frames of exactly that. *Here:* Rook's Ready (sword up by his right shoulder), Raised (overhead, blade down his back) and Strike (both hands low on his left, the arc from his upper right), from Mark's kendo and PS4 references (2026-10-01).
- **Line of action (weight shift).** The one curve or slant the whole body follows in a pose; animators draw it first. A swing reads as powerful when the body leans with it: coiled away on the way up, thrown into the cut on the way down, the shoulders tilting, the feet turning with the hips. *Here:* Rook's strike poses lean from the hips (right as he raises the sword, left as he cuts), the head half as far, and his feet turn with them (Mark, 2026-10-01, from Chaz's slash in Phantasy Star IV).
- **Pixel lines at any angle.** A thin line drawn by filling a shape thins to a stair-step on the diagonals, so a sword looks thinner in some frames than others. Pixel artists draw a line one pixel per step along its longer axis, and thicken it along its shorter axis, so it keeps one weight at every angle. *Here:* Rook's katana (`katana()` in `src/art/rig2/battle.ts`).
- **Athletic (ready) stance.** Knees bent, feet a little wider than the hips, weight low: a fighter ready to move. From behind it mostly reads as the body sitting lower and the shins leaning out to wider-set feet. *Here:* the battle backs' `stance` (Rook's coat comes down over his boots and his feet spread), in place of the old Crouch slider that slid the whole figure down, feet and all.
- **Keyframes and in-betweens (tweening).** The important poses of a move are keyframes; the frames between them are in-betweens, drawn by an assistant in hand animation or computed by code from the keyframes' joint angles. *Here:* Mark sets key poses in the editor; code makes the in-betweens.
- **Onion skinning.** Showing the previous (and next) frame faintly under the one being drawn, so motion can be judged against it. Named after animators' thin onion-skin paper. *Here:* the editor's reference overlay.
- **Animation tags (named frame ranges).** Keeping all of a character's frames in one strip and naming ranges of it (idle, wind-up, strike), each with a play direction: forward, reverse, or ping-pong (there and back). Aseprite works this way ([tags](https://www.aseprite.org/docs/tags/)). RPG Maker's battler sheets are the simplest case: three frames per motion, a loop plays 1-2-3-2 (ping-pong) and a one-shot plays 1-2-3 (forward) ([MZ help](https://rpgmakerofficial.com/product/MZ_help-en/01_11_02.html)). *Here:* the planned Animation Composer for Sprite Fusion frames (`docs/TOOLING-UI.md` §4.2), 2026-10-02.
- **Event track (hit markers).** A row on an animation timeline that holds markers instead of pictures: "the blow lands here", "play this sound", "shake the screen". The hit becomes data at an exact frame rather than a number buried in code. Godot's version deliberately does not fire in the editor's preview, so previewing never causes side effects ([Godot track types](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/animation/animation_track_types.html)). *Here:* the composer's Events lane, which previews flashes and shakes but never applies damage; see also "Timeline keyed to the hit" above.
- **Visemes.** The handful of mouth shapes speech is made of (closed for m/b/p, wide for "ee", round for "oo"...). Lip-sync picks one per sound; cheap lip flap uses just open and closed. *Here:* PixelLab offers viseme sets; our portraits use open/closed (`docs/PIXELLAB-LESSONS.md`).
- **Lip flap and blinks.** The cheapest life a talking portrait can have: the mouth opens and closes while the text types (not synced to real speech, just a beat), and the eyes shut for a few frames now and then. Two extra frames per face, timed by code. *Here:* rig v2 portraits (`src/art/rig2/portrait.ts`, played by `src/scenes/dialog.ts`) since 2026-09-30.
- **Expression sheets.** A portrait drawn once and its face changed per emotion (happy, sad, angry, surprised...). At small sizes an expression is mostly eyes, brows and mouth, often only a pixel or two moved: a lid lowered for a smirk, the inner brow corners dropped for anger, closed arcs for a smile. *Here:* the art pass redrew whole faces for the main speakers; code draws the rest that way.
- **Idle animation.** Small motion while standing still (breathing, bouncing) so the scene doesn't look frozen. *Here:* Kit bounces; party members breathe in battle.
- **Paper-doll (modular) characters.** A character assembled at runtime from layered parts (body, head, hair, hat, top, legs) drawn to line up on a shared body template, so a few dozen parts make thousands of people. Used by Stardew Valley's farmer, RPG Maker's generator and the open "LPC" sprite sets. The engine is the easy part; the art is the hard part: every part needs every facing and walk frame, all aligned to the same anchor points, with the layer order changing by facing (hair goes behind the head when walking away). *Here:* the game's own procedural characters already work this way (`src/art/chars.ts`: letter-grid body, hair and accessory layers through a palette; `randomLook(seed)` for crowds). Discussed 2026-09-30 as a way to vary townsfolk made with PixelLab.
- **Palette swap (recolouring).** Drawing a sprite once with its colours in known slots (skin, hair, top, trousers) and swapping the slots at runtime for new colours, for variety at no art cost. Classic for enemies and crowds. It needs each colour slot to be known, which is easy for art made that way and takes a labelling step for AI art.
- **Layer order (z-order).** Which parts draw over which. It changes with facing and with motion (an arm in front of the body on one step, behind it on the next), which is one of the things AI-generated animation gets wrong from frame to frame.
- **Anchoring a frame (the pivot).** Each frame of an animation sits on a canvas of its own size, so the engine needs one fixed point to stand it on: usually the midpoint between the feet and the soles' row. If frames are anchored by their canvas edge instead, a sprite that leans or swings a sword slides along the ground. *Here:* `anchored` in `rig2/sfcrew.ts` cuts every frame of a loop onto equal-width canvases centred on the mean feet midpoint.
- **Anchoring by the planted foot.** When an animation changes pose a lot (an upright wind-up, then a deep lunge), the stand-on point matters more than the canvas: pick the foot that stays planted (in a kendo men-uchi, the front foot that stamps) and put it on the same pixel in every frame. The rest of the body then moves around a fixed foot, which reads as a lunge, where a feet-midpoint anchor would slide the stamping foot. Measure it once and record it as data, with a test that re-measures the art. *Here:* `SF_ANCHORS` and `buildSfStrike` in `rig2/sfstrike.ts`.
- **Missing in-betweens and smear-as-blur.** When the animator (or the AI) has made only the first and last pose of a fast move, the middle can be faked in code for a frame or two: the body of one pose, the blade drawn by code at an angle between, and a crescent (the swept arc) that stands for the blurred blade. The eye accepts it because the move is only a few frames long, but it is a stand-in; a real in-between frame is better. *Here:* round 2 bent two stand-in frames in `rig2/sfstrike.ts`; round 3 replaced them with a swipe (below).
- **Bending a frame by rows (stand-in in-betweens).** When only the first and last pose of a move exist, a middle frame can be made from one of them by deleting a few rows of the legs (everything above drops, so the knees bend and the feet stay put) and sliding each row sideways in proportion to its height (the torso tips over the front foot). Rows move whole, so no pixel is blended or resampled. A held weapon is turned separately about the hands. It is a cheap stand-in for one or two frames at speed, not a replacement for a real drawing. *Here:* `bend()` and the `SF_POSES` table in `src/art/rig2/sfstrike.ts` (Rook's dip, swing A, swing M and recover).
- **Facing and mirroring.** Side-view sprites face one way, and the other way is usually a flip. A flip is free for a symmetric figure and wrong for an asymmetric one (Rook's chrome arm would change sides), so Sprite Fusion sprites all face right and the layout is built around that: party left, enemies right. *Here:* the party is mirrored out of the old layout, and Sable, who has no art yet, is the one flipped sprite.
- **Depth rows (front row and back row).** Characters in a 2D battle do not shrink with distance, so depth is only shown by height on the screen and by who is drawn over whom: the lower a sprite's feet, the nearer it is, and it is drawn last. A small creature in a front row is never hidden by the big ones behind it. *Here:* the Glowrat stands nine rows lower than the punks when the group is too wide for one row, and the party's line climbs from Kit (nearest) up toward the back. A side effect: with no scaling, a big depth range looks like characters standing on the pavement edge.
- **Easing a stop.** Moving at a constant speed and halting in one frame reads as a slide, so the last stretch slows down (ease-out), and a character often changes pose as it stops (a skid). *Here:* the walk-in's last 24 pixels slow to a quarter of the speed and Kit drops into her stance.
- **Keep-out rectangles and layout tests.** Instead of checking a layout by eye, write the rule down as numbers (this sprite's widest reach, a margin, the menu's rectangle) and test it against the real art, so a later change that breaks it fails a test. *Here:* `tests/sflayout.test.ts`.
- **Combo of blows from one action (cosmetic hits).** A three-hit combo can still be one move to the rules: one damage roll, one number. The extra blows are drawn (flash, spark, shove, a short hitstop each) so the player sees three, and the real damage lands with the last. The pose and the script both count in animation frames from the first blow, so every frame lines up with its spark. *Here:* Kit's jab, cross and kick (`punchCombo` in `playback.ts`, `punchHits` in `rig2/sfpunch.ts`); the script throws the first blow but does NOT play its spark yet: it waits for the engine to roll hit or miss, then a hit plays every blow and a miss plays a whiff (a dull puff short of the target, no star) and stops, so a player never sees a clean hit and then MISS. The blows are rows of a table (`PUNCH_BLOW`, `multiHit`), kept on the actor (`followUp`), not in a module variable.
- **Hit-confirm anchor (landing every blow on one spot).** When different frames of a combo (a punch, a different punch, a kick) have to hit the same place, place each frame by its blow's tip, not by its feet: the kick's toe is laid on the column the fist ended on, so the target is hit in one spot however the legs move. The planted boot anchors the punches (they never slide); the toe anchors the kick. *Here:* `PUNCH_ANCHORS` and `tipDx` in `rig2/sfpunch.ts`.
- **Smear frame versus held frame.** A smear frame (a streak over the arm, a crescent behind the leg) shows the moment of speed for one frame; the held frame is the clean pose that stays through the impact and hitstop. A blow is: smear, then impact on the first held frame. *Here:* `jabS`/`jab`, `crossS`/`cross`, `kickS`/`kick`.
- **Recoil, chamber and settle (the frames around a blow).** A blow reads as part of a flow when the limb comes back before the next one goes out (recoil), the body prepares the heavy move (a chamber: the knee up before a kick) and eases down after it (a settle). Without them a combo is poses with flashes. A small forward shove of the whole sprite on each blow, growing from jab to kick, adds weight. *Here:* the load frame as the coil between the jab and the cross, `kickB`/`kickC`/`kickD`/`kickE` (Mark's kick with its leg re-posed in code: see two-bone leg below) as the chamber and the drop, `PUNCH_PUSH`.
- **Lane and depth sorting in a side view.** Characters stand on rows; one with a lower row (nearer the camera) is drawn over one with a higher row. A character running past an enemy should take a row in front of it, not the same row, or the two read as merged. *Here:* `PUNCH_LANE_AHEAD` puts Kit's soles 10 world px below the feet of any enemy her run passes (round 4; was 7), and she is drawn over them at full opacity. Two same-size sprites in neighbouring lanes still overlap in 2D, which is normal in a side view and reads as depth; round 3 faded the enemy instead and the judges read the ghost as a render bug (see below).
- **Two-bone limb re-pose from one drawn frame (rotate about the hip, then the knee).** A limb is two rigid pieces and a joint. To get an in-between pose without redrawing, cut the limb out of the frame by its band, turn the thigh about the hip, turn the shin about the knee on top of that, and sample each output pixel from the original through the inverse turns (nine sub-samples, commonest colour, so no new colour and no stair-step). A plain rotation leaves a notch on the outside of the joint and a dark seam on the inside, so clean the joint: fill the gap with the jeans colour, outline the new edge, and drop warm rim-light flecks and one-pixel spikes. *Here:* `poseLeg` in `rig2/sfpunch.ts` makes Kit's knee-up chamber and her foot-drop from Mark's kick.
- **Fading what the hero passes (see-through occlusion).** In a side view you cannot route a runner around another figure of the same size without the two silhouettes merging for a few frames. The cheap, readable answer is to fade the figure she overlaps to about 40 percent while the overlap lasts, computed each frame from the two sprites' opaque spans (nothing to reset when she goes home). *Here:* `punchFade` in `battlekit/render.ts`, `PUNCH_FADE`. *Round 4 verdict:* it did not work here. A see-through enemy standing behind the hero for most of a combo looks like a bug, not like depth. Draw order (the hero in front, opaque) plus a lane a few pixels lower is the standard answer; fade only for something that blocks the view for a moment, like a wall.
- **Holding the pose until the game answers (hold versus hitstop).** Hitstop freezes everything for a few frames AFTER a hit. A hold is the other direction: the attacker's pose stops at full reach BEFORE the hit is confirmed, because the game does not yet know if it landed (here, a timing ring waits for a press and the engine only answers after the late window). Held at the moment of contact it reads as a deliberate beat, where letting the animation run on would pull the fist back before the spark. *Here:* `Disp.poseHold` in `battlekit/types.ts`, set and released in `multiHit` (`battlekit/playback.ts`); `update` in `scenes/battle.ts` stops counting the pose down while it is set. The better fix is to roll hit or miss before the ring opens, which is an engine change.
- **Spark size ladder (escalating impact within a combo).** In a three-blow combo the player should feel each blow is bigger than the last: the first star about the size of the fist, the second one and a half times that, the last the biggest, plus longer hitstop on the heavy ones. Small sparks also have to be gone before the next wind-up starts or they hide the target and the anticipation of the next move. *Here:* `PUNCH_BLOW` (power, hit pause) and `chunkStar` in `battle/fx.ts`; sparks of the first two blows last about 4 to 6 frames.
- **Whiff versus hit feedback.** A miss should look different from a hit before the number says so: no star, no shake, no hit pause, a small dull puff and an air-cut short of the target, and the target steps out of the line. The game logic has to decide hit or miss first and the visuals follow. *Here:* the `punch_whiff` effect in `battle/fx.ts` and `multiHit` in `playback.ts`.
- **Stale rate bug (reading a clock before it updates).** A script converted animation frames to real frames using the scene's frame rate from the start of the tick, but a held confirm key that the same tick armed made it 1.6x for one tick, so a pose timeline came out too long and the hit fired before the fist arrived. The fix is to refresh the rate at the moment it is read (`anim` in `battle.ts`). A lesson for any code that turns durations into frames: read the clock when you use it.
- **Axis and per-frame offset (lining up frames of different sizes).** Fighting-game engines store an *axis* with every sprite: the one pixel it stands on, usually at the feet. A frame of an animation is then a sprite plus a small offset from that axis, so pictures of any canvas size line up on the same ground point without being padded to one size. MUGEN works this way ([AIR docs](https://elecbyte.com/mugendocs/air.html)); OpenBOR, which uses one offset for a whole animation, warns that mismatched canvases make a character shake or slide ([OpenBOR wiki](https://chronocrash.com/openbor/wiki/index.php/Animation_Overview)). The axis says where the picture stands; the offset is a deliberate shift for one frame (a lean, a hop). *Here:* the Animation Composer's crosshair, so Rook's 69x110 wind-up and 101x66 follow-through share a ground point (`docs/TOOLING-UI.md` §4.2), 2026-10-02.
- **Ticks (one unit for timing).** A tick is one step of a fixed game clock, 1/60 of a second in MUGEN, which counts every frame's hold in ticks ([AIR docs](https://elecbyte.com/mugendocs/air.html)). Authoring every duration in one unit matters: moves carried from MUGEN to OpenBOR, which defaults to centiseconds, had to be retimed by hand ([ChronoCrash](https://www.chronocrash.com/forum/threads/converting-mugen-chars-to-openbor.1074/page-2)). *Here:* the composer authors holds in 60 Hz ticks with milliseconds beside them, and the battle converts to its own pose pace once (see "Pose frames are not 60 fps frames").
- **Movement as frame data (a lunge on the timeline).** Instead of code sliding the attacker toward the target, each frame of the move says how far the fighter travels on it (sideways, and in depth). The lunge's speed and its stop are then visible and editable next to the poses. OpenBOR's `move` and `movez` frame commands work this way ([OpenBOR animations](https://chronocrash.com/obor/wiki/animations-overview/)). Movement is kept apart from the picture's offset, so the shadow and the contact point travel with the fighter. *Here:* the composer's Move lane, 2026-10-02; today's version is code (`reachX/reachY` in `playback.ts`).
- **Effects as spawned objects, not drawn into frames.** A hit spark, a smear or a dust puff is its own short-lived object, started at a chosen tick and placed relative to the character's axis or the point of contact, with its own draw depth. Baking it into the sprite would tie it to one frame's size and stop it from overlapping the target properly. OpenBOR spawns its hit flash as a separate model at the contact point ([Hit Effects](https://chronocrash.com/openbor/wiki/index.php/Hit_Effects)). *Here:* the composer's Effects lane, picking from the code swipes and FX lab presets.

## Tiles and maps

- **Tile.** A small square of ground art (16×16 here) repeated on a grid to build a level.
- **Autotiling / Wang tiles.** A tile set built so two terrains can meet in any shape: each tile's four corners are labelled with a terrain, and a map picks, for every cell, the tile whose corners match its neighbours. Two terrains × four corners = 16 tiles. Named after mathematician Hao Wang, who studied edge-matching tiles in 1961. Also called autotiles, "blob" sets (a 47-tile edge-and-corner variant) or marching squares. *Here:* the art pass's terrain options; `src/dev/artswap.ts` lays them into real maps.
- **Baking.** Drawing a level's static parts (ground, buildings, props) once into big images when the map loads, instead of redrawing them every frame. *Here:* `src/field/fieldmap.ts`.
- **Swipe (a fan of speed instead of a stand-in pose).** When the in-between frame does not exist, the cheapest honest answer is to draw the motion, not the pose: a drawn blade at where it is and a thin crescent of steel trailing it (a bright rim, a lighter band and a steel band, thin at the trailing end; round 3's dithered fan read as speckle noise next to smooth-shaded art), over the real poses on either side, so the eye reads one swing and the join between the poses is hidden. It is data (hands, two angles, reach, width), and no real pixel is stretched. A swipe also has to fit the screen: the blade swings in a plane tipped toward the viewer, so its reach straight up is shorter than sideways (an ellipse), which keeps it clear of the name plate. *Here:* `SF_SWIPES`, `drawSwipe` in `rig2/sfstrike.ts`.
- **Passing through an ally.** In a side view the attacker's lunge usually crosses a teammate who stands in front. Real games either draw the attacker on top and let them overlap for a few frames (Chrono Trigger), or move the ally. Ducking the ally reads as 'she was hit'. Walking her through his empty place hid her for 4 to 8 frames (round 3); the cheaper answer is a half step into the second row (up the street, a little left) before the dash starts, tied to the beat, so he passes in front and her head and torso stay in view. It cannot be zero overlap in a tight staircase; count the overlapping pixels each tick instead of judging by eye. *Here:* `makeRoom` in `battlekit/render.ts`, `SF_ROOM`.
- **Pose frames are not 60 fps frames.** An engine can run its animation on its own clock (here a 'pace' of 0.65 per tick), so '2 frames' of a pose is about 26 ms, not 17. Quote a hold in pose frames and in milliseconds, and measure at the game's own tick. *Here:* `FX_PACE` in `scenes/battle.ts`.
- **Hard colour swap for a hit blink.** A hit shown as a translucent white laid over the sprite reads as a ghost (it fades the colours). The old-console way is a swap: for one or two frames every pixel of the sprite becomes one flat light colour, the dark outline stays, and nothing is blended, so the silhouette stays crisp. Through a hitstop it alternates on and off so the sprite is seen between blinks. *Here:* `flatLight` in `battlekit/render.ts`.
- **Charge by rising.** A held wind-up pose feels frozen; a wind-up that is the body slowly standing up out of a crouch (two or three steps) feels like gathering force, and costs fewer held frames. *Here:* `dip`, `riseA`, `rise`, `windup` in `rig2/sfstrike.ts`; the whole lead is 17 pose frames (435 ms).
- **Constant-rate dash.** Frames in a fast move should cover about the same distance each, or one frame looks like a jump. Plan the per-frame positions as a list and check the largest step. *Here:* `sfTimeline`'s lunge values.
- **Depth sorting (y-sorting).** Drawing things lower on screen last, so a character walking behind a lamp post is hidden by it and one in front covers it. *Here:* tall props become depth-sorted sprites.
- **Emissive layer.** A second copy of the art holding only what glows (neon, lamps, eyes), drawn un-darkened over a night scene. *Here:* every baked map has one; bloom reads from it.

## Rendering and effects

- **Frame budget.** At 60 frames a second each frame has 16.7 ms for everything: game logic, drawing and handing the picture to the screen. Going over drops frames and feels like stutter. *Here:* `e2e/perf.spec.ts` gates it.
- **Post-processing.** Effects applied to the whole finished picture: bloom, colour grading, distortion. *Here:* the WebGL presenter (`src/engine/gl/presenter.ts`).
- **Bloom.** Bright things bleed a soft glow into their surroundings, like a camera lens. *Here:* neon, lamps and spells.
- **Light bleeding onto sprites (and cutting it out).** Bloom is added over the whole finished picture, so a bright sign behind a character also lights the character's pixels, and grey hair in front of a pink sign turns pink. The fix is to cut the character's silhouette out of the light layer before it is blurred, so only light that is in front of or beside them can bleed. *Here:* `partyCuts` in `src/scenes/battlekit/render.ts`.
- **Chromatic aberration (colour split).** Separating the red, green and blue channels a little, like a cheap lens; used for a jolt on big hits.
- **Shockwave.** A ring that distorts the picture outward from an impact.
- **Particles and emitters.** Many tiny sprites (sparks, embers, dust) spawned by an *emitter* with rules for speed, spread, gravity, lifetime and colour. *Here:* presets in `src/data/fx.json`, tuned in the FX lab.
- **Anticipation (charge-up).** A beat before the action that tells the player something big is coming: power gathering in a hand, a crouch, a glint. It makes the hit land harder because the eye was ready for it. *Here:* every spell's `cast.<fx>` moment gathers particles inward at the caster through the windup (2026-10-01); Rook's sword goes up over his head (his Raised pose) before it comes down.
- **Heat haze (refraction).** Hot air bends light, so whatever is behind it shimmers. In a game it's a small wavering distortion of the picture over a patch, drifting upward. *Here:* over Firebrand's and Wildfire's flames (`postfx.haze`).
- **Stage dim.** Darkening everything except the effect for the length of a big spell, so its light owns the screen. A JRPG staple for summons and ultimates. *Here:* Wildfire, Overload and Dragon Coil (`postfx.dim`; whatever glows stays lit).
- **Glitch effect.** Slices of the picture sliding sideways with the colour channels split, the look of corrupted video. *Here:* where Hex's hacks land (`postfx.glitch`).
- **Hitstop.** Freezing the action for a few frames when a blow lands, so it feels heavy. A fighting-game staple.
- **Hit flash on the target.** A short white (or bright) blink of the struck sprite's own pixels at the moment of impact, often held through the hitstop, so the freeze reads as 'this took the hit'. It is applied to the target, never the attacker, and the detail stays visible under it. *Here:* `dd.flash` on the enemy in `playback.ts`, the enemy draw in `render.ts`.
- **Screen shake and flash.** Shaking or flashing the screen for impact. Used sparingly, and turned down for comfort (the Options menu has settings for both).
- **Software vs hardware rendering.** Graphics drawn by the GPU (hardware) versus emulated on the CPU (software). The same effect can run at 60 fps on a GPU and 25 fps in software. *Here:* CI's machines have no GPU, which is why the game refuses software WebGL (2026-09-30).

## Battle design (JRPG)

- **The JRPG loop.** Explore → fight → earn XP and money → level up and buy gear → face harder places. Phantasy Star IV's version is the model for this game.
- **Turn order.** Who acts when. *Here:* a turn strip shows the order.
- **Resources (MP/TP).** A pool abilities spend. *Here:* each member has their own (KI, RAM, MANA), and Rook uses limited skill charges instead.
- **Elements and weaknesses.** Damage types (FIRE, SHOCK, MANA) that some enemies take extra or less damage from, rewarding the right tool for the job.
- **Telegraph (tell).** A warning an enemy gives before a big move, so the player can react (guard, interrupt). *Here:* boss tells pinned on screen until acted on.
- **Timed hits (action commands).** Pressing a button on a beat during an attack or defence for a bonus, as in Super Mario RPG and Paper Mario. *Here:* the strike, brace and mend rings.
- **Combos (combination techniques).** Two or three party members' moves fusing into one stronger move when ordered together, as in Phantasy Star IV and Chrono Trigger. *Here:* Clean Job, Thunder Rift and the rest.
- **Targeting AI / aggro.** How enemies choose whom to hit. Pure random feels unfair one way, always-the-weakest feels unfair the other. *Here:* "smell blood" weights the lowest-HP member 1.5×, likely but not certain.
- **Palette-swapped enemies.** Reusing one enemy sprite in new colours for a stronger or different enemy (see *Palette swap* above), a classic way to stretch art. *Here:* a second copy of an enemy in one fight is shifted in colour so they read as different.
- **Encounter rate.** How often random battles trigger while walking. Too high is tiring. *Here:* about 1 in 40 steps in the Sinkline.
- **Startup, active and recovery (frame data).** Fighting games describe every move by three spans: *startup*, the time before the first frame that can hit; *active*, the frames that can hit; and *recovery*, the time until the attacker can act again. Around them sit hitstun (how long the defender is locked after being hit), a cancel window (how long after contact another move can cut in) and frame advantage (who recovers first) ([Dustloop](https://dustloop.com/w/Using_Frame_Data)). A turn-based game doesn't need the advantage maths, but the three spans still answer how long until the blow lands and how long until the attacker is back home. *Here:* the Animation Composer works them out from the frames and the hit marker and shows them as a bar (`docs/TOOLING-UI.md` §4.2), 2026-10-02.
- **Hitbox and hurtbox.** In a real-time fighter each frame carries boxes: an attack box (hitbox) where the blow can connect and a body box (hurtbox) where the character can be struck; a hit happens when one side's attack box overlaps the other's body box. MUGEN calls them Clsn1 and Clsn2 ([AIR docs](https://elecbyte.com/mugendocs/air.html)). IKEMEN GO adds a depth to attacks (`attack.depth`) so a blow in a 2.5D scene can't hit someone far in front or behind ([Character features](https://github.com/ikemen-engine/Ikemen-GO/wiki/Character-features)). *Here:* not used, because a turn-based battle already knows who is being hit; a move's hit marker carries a contact point and a reach in depth rows instead.

- **Readable damage numbers (outline, drop shadow, tint).** A number that pops over a fight must read on any ground, a white boss included. The recipe is a dark outline two pixels thick round the letters (one pixel vanishes on a bright body), a soft drop shadow under that, and a tint that says what kind of hit it was: pale for an ordinary hit, amber for a critical, cyan for a weak spot. The number also floats clear of the slash effect so the two never fight for the same pixels. *Here:* `NUMBER_LOOK` and `HIT_COLOUR` in `src/stage/hudcolours.ts` / `hudkit.ts`, used by the lab's still number and the Battle Test's floating one.
- **See-through panels (alpha gradient).** A HUD window that is partly transparent lets the scene show through, so the HUD feels part of the screen and the stage reads as bigger. Making the top edge more see-through than the bottom keeps text on the darker part where it sits. *Here:* panels are about 68% opaque at the top and 80% at the bottom (`SEE_THROUGH_TOP` and each box's `opacity`).
- **Health states and the low-health warning.** A bar that is always green says nothing until the last moment. The usual states are green, then amber under half, then red under a quarter, and under a quarter the bar (and often the number) blinks so a hero in trouble is the first thing the eye lands on. *Here:* `hpColor` and `LOW_HP` in the HUD; the blink follows the stage's fixed tick.
- **Turn-order preview.** A timeline that shows only this round leaves most of its track empty late in a round. Many games show the next round's order after a divider, dimmed, as a forecast (it can change if speeds change). *Here:* the timeline's tick and dim chips after it, built from the same order list.
- **Status icons.** A tiny picture per condition (poison, stun, guard, a stat buff) beside a character's row, so a glance says who is affected without opening a menu. Icons are limited to the most urgent few. *Here:* `src/stage/hudstatus.ts`, 5x5 pixel pictures written out as text.
- **Number size by hit type.** Games make the number itself carry the weight of the blow: an ordinary hit is a modest size, a critical or weak-spot hit is clearly bigger, so a combo reads as a sequence of small and big beats. With pixel fonts the sizes must be whole multiples (3x and 4x of the 5 px glyph), because a fraction would smear the pixels. *Here:* `numberScale` in `hudcolours.ts`.
- **Ghosting a forecast (alpha, not just dimming).** To mark a thing as "not real yet", draw it see-through rather than only darker: the panel behind shows through it, so it cannot be mistaken for a live item. *Here:* the timeline's next-round chips are drawn at half strength.
- **Colour for kind, shape for identity (status icons).** At 5 px a picture cannot say much, so the colour carries the family (buff arrows cyan, debuff arrows red, poison purple, fire orange) and the silhouette says which one inside the family (each stat has its own arrow). Fighting games and ARPGs do the same with buff and debuff bars. *Here:* `STATUS_LOOK` in `hudstatus.ts`.
- **Label on the thing, not only in the list.** When two enemies are identical the list says "A" and "B" but the player's eyes are on the stage, so the letter must also hang on the sprite (here, beside its health bar). The same field feeds every place it appears so they cannot disagree. *Here:* `HudFoeView.tag`, drawn by the timeline chip, the foe list, the target box and the stage's bar.
- **Contrast ratio (WCAG).** A number for how readable text is on its ground: lighter divided by darker luminance, each with 0.05 added. 4.5:1 is the usual floor for small text; HUD text read in a glance wants about 7:1. A unit test can check a palette against it. *Here:* the contrast test in `tests/stagehudpolish.test.ts`.
- **Outline for text on a lit ground.** Text over a bright or coloured highlight (the acting hero's lit row) loses contrast however you tune the colour, so each letter gets a one-pixel dark outline, the way subtitles do. *Here:* the party table's active row.

- **Non-integer pixel scaling.** Enlarging pixel art by 1.25x or 1.5x makes some pixels one screen pixel wide and some two, so the grain looks uneven; blurring it instead would break the palette. A cleaner way is an area vote: each output pixel takes the colour that covers most of the patch of the source it stands for. *Here:* `stretchTo` in `src/art/rig2/enemy.ts` scales the enemies to party size for the side-view spike; the real fix is art drawn at the right size.

## Frameworks and the Phaser stage spike

- **Game framework versus game engine.** A *framework* (Phaser) is a library of code you call from your own program: it draws sprites, plays animations, loads files and reads the keyboard, and you write the game around it. An *engine with an editor* (Godot, Unity, RPG Maker) is a whole application with windows to place things in and a runtime behind them. A framework gives you the plumbing and no editor; if you want editing tools, you build them (or add a community one). *Here:* the Phaser spike (`src/stage/`, `docs/spikes/phaser-stage.md`) asks whether building the battle stage on a framework makes our own design tools easier to build.
- **Scene life cycle.** A scene is one screen of a Phaser game, and Phaser calls its methods in a fixed order: `init` (receive data), `preload` (queue the files to download), `create` (build the objects once everything has arrived), then `update` every frame. Restarting a scene runs the same four again on the same object, so anything the scene remembers has to be reset in `init`. *Here:* `StageScene` in `src/stage/stagescene.ts`.
- **Fixed timestep (the accumulator).** Screens refresh at 60, 120 or 144 Hz, but game rules must advance at one steady rate or a fast monitor would play the game faster. The loop adds the real time since the last frame to a running total and runs one fixed 1/60-second step for every full step the total holds, keeping the remainder; after a stall it drops the backlog rather than replaying it. *Here:* `StageScene.update` runs `tick()` this way, ready to feed the frame-counted battle engine, and the main game loop (`src/main.ts`) does the same.
- **Texture filtering (nearest versus linear).** When a picture is drawn bigger than it is stored, the graphics card must invent in-between colours. *Linear* blends neighbours (smooth, and blurry for pixel art); *nearest* copies the closest source pixel (hard square pixels). Pixel art wants nearest, plus a whole-number zoom, so every source pixel becomes the same size block. *Here:* the Phaser game is created with `pixelArt: true`, every texture is also marked NEAREST, and a Playwright test checks that every 4 x 4 block of a zoom-4 screenshot is a single colour.
- **Texture from a canvas.** A canvas is a drawing surface the code can paint on; a graphics framework can adopt it as a texture, uploaded once to the graphics card and then shared by any number of sprites. That is how art that is generated in code (backdrops, enemies) can be used by a framework without exporting images. Add each canvas once under a stable name, or the same picture is uploaded again on every rebuild (a texture leak). *Here:* `src/stage/textures.ts`.
- **A stage as data: depth rows and slots.** Rather than writing "put the hero at x=98, y=201" in code, the stage keeps a list of depth rows (a foot height each, back to front) and of slots (a row and an x), and each fighter is just assigned a slot. Moving a fighter to another row, or changing a row's height, is then a change to a number in a file, which is exactly what a visual editor edits. *Here:* `src/data/stages.json` (street and sewer, the final design: five rows, party slots in a diagonal column, an enemy slot set for each of 1 to 6 enemies and for a boss with 0, 1 or 2 helpers) and `src/stage/config.ts`.
- **Z axis and the walkable depth band.** A 2.5D brawler gives every character three numbers: x across the screen, z into the screen (depth), and an altitude for jumps. The floor is not a 3D space but a band of screen rows: OpenBOR's `z zmin zmax` says how high up and how far down characters may walk, counted in pixels from the top of the screen, and modders measure their stage picture to find those rows ([OpenBOR axis wiki](https://chronocrash.com/obor/wiki/axis/), [ChronoCrash forum](https://chronocrash.com/forum/threads/z-vs-panel-interaction.8250/latest)). Coming toward the camera moves a character down the screen and in front of others; jumping changes only the altitude, so the shadow stays on the floor. *Here:* `floor.y0` (equal to the horizon) and `floor.y1` in `src/data/stages.json` are the band, the depth rows inside it are the z values, and the Battle Stage Editor drags both (`docs/TOOLING-UI.md` §3.4, §6.1), 2026-10-02.
- **Sort point and sorting group (why y-sorting needs the feet).** Y-sorting compares one point per sprite, and engines take that point from the sprite's position or pivot, which defaults to its centre in Unity ([Unity 2D sorting](https://docs.unity3d.com/2023.1/Documentation/Manual/2DSorting.html)) and to its origin in Godot ([KidsCanCode](https://kidscancode.org/godot_recipes/4.x/2d/using_ysort/index.html)). A tall sprite sorted by its centre gets drawn behind a short one standing nearer, so the point must be moved to the feet. A *sorting group* makes a figure built from parts (body, weapon, shadow) sort as one, and a manual order (Tiled's "index" mode, [Tiled forum](https://discourse.mapeditor.org/t/objectgroup-rendering-order/1586)) fixes the rare tie that sorts the wrong way. *Here:* `depthFor` in `src/stage/config.ts` sorts by foot y from each sprite's measured foot (`src/stage/feet.ts`); the stage editor adds a forward and back override within a row.
- **Parallax layers.** Background layers that move at different speeds as the camera moves (the far wall slowly, the near floor faster, foreground rails fastest) so a flat picture reads as deep. Each layer gets a ratio: 1 moves with the camera, less drifts behind it. OpenBOR gives every background and foreground layer separate x and z ratios ([fglayer thread](https://www.chronocrash.com/forum/threads/fglayer.1803/latest)). *Here:* proposed per battleback layer; it only shows once a move pans or pushes the battle camera.
- **Device pixels versus CSS pixels (crisp pixel art on a scaled display).** A web page counts in CSS pixels, but the monitor has physical (device) pixels, and a Windows display set to 125% has 1.25 of them per CSS pixel. A canvas "zoomed 2x" in CSS pixels is then 2.5 device pixels per game pixel, so some game pixels get 2 device pixels and some 3 (an uneven shimmer). The fix is to count in device pixels: pick a whole number k of device pixels per game pixel, then ask the page for k divided by the pixel ratio in CSS pixels. Browsers also lay things out in 1/64 of a CSS pixel, so a size or offset that is not a multiple of that is rounded and the edge can blend; the stage nudges its margin to one that is exact. *Here:* `src/stage/zoom.ts` (the maths, tested at 100, 125, 150, 200 and 250%) and `centreOnDevicePixels` in `src/stage/boot.ts`; `e2e/stagelab-dpr.spec.ts` checks every game pixel is an exact k by k block. The game's own `src/engine/display.ts` solves the same problem its own way.
- **Frames from the tick, not from the animation clock (determinism).** An engine's built-in animation player advances on real time, so two runs of the same fight drift apart, and a hit-pause cannot freeze it. If the picture is chosen from the fixed-step tick counter instead (`frame = floor(tick * fps / 60) + phase`, modulo the frame count), the same tick always shows the same picture: a replay is repeatable, a test can jump to any moment by stepping N ticks, and hitstop is just "skip the tick for a few frames" so every figure holds still together. *Here:* `idleFrame` in `src/stage/idle.ts` and `StageScene.step` / `tick`.
- **Pixel-perfect picking and snapping while dragging.** Clicking a sprite normally tests its whole picture rectangle, so the empty corner of a cell would grab it too; a pixel-perfect hit test looks up the drawn pixel's alpha, so only the visible figure can be picked. While dragging, the sprite does not follow the pointer freely: the foot position snaps to the nearest depth row and the x is kept on the fighter's own half of the screen, the way RPG Maker snaps a troop member to its grid. *Here:* `StageScene.setEditMode` (`pixelPerfect: true, draggable: true`) and the pure `snapSlot` in `src/stage/config.ts`.
- **Texture lifetime: add once, remember on the texture, release what nobody shows.** A texture made from generated art costs a generator run plus an upload, so it is created once under a stable key and every later request finds it by name. What was learned about it (where a sprite's feet are, a backdrop's light colour) can be stored on the texture itself in Phaser's `customData`, so a scene restart repeats no work. Textures made on demand (one shadow ellipse per size) are released when no object uses them any more, or a size slider would leave one behind for every step. *Here:* `registerCrew`, `addBackdrop`, `addEnemy`, `pruneShadows` in `src/stage/textures.ts`.
- **Moving a horizon on a baked backdrop.** *(Round 2 of the spike; replaced in P2.)* A backdrop painted as one picture has its wall/floor line at a fixed row, so round 2 cut it into named frames and stretched a one-pixel strip over any gap a dragged horizon opened. Once the floor is painted from the config (next entry), the horizon is just a number the painter reads: dragging it repaints the picture (about 10 ms) and nothing is stretched.

- **Painting a floor to fit a camera (growing bands, upright joints).** A floor seen from above at an angle needs only two cues to read as a receding surface: stripes that get taller toward you, and joints that lean slightly toward a far-away point. Equal stripes look flat, and joints meeting at a vanishing point in the middle of the picture say "a road running away", which contradicts fighters standing in a line across the screen. So the bands grow by a fixed ratio (1.2x each), and the paving joints are drawn as if they met 400 pixels ABOVE the screen, so they lean only a little. A brighter seam on each band's top edge, stronger toward the camera, finishes the effect. *Here:* `paintFloor` in `src/stage/floor.ts`, every number in `floor` of the stage config.
- **Ordered dithering as a fade (the Bayer matrix).** To fade smoothly with a limited palette, compare a strength from 0 to 1 with a fixed 4x4 pattern of thresholds: a pixel changes only where the pattern value is below the strength. 0.25 changes a quarter of the pixels in an evenly scattered way, 0.5 half, and the eye blends the result into a gradient. It is the same fade everywhere (haze, neon spill, seams, the sky fade), so the whole picture shares one grain. *Here:* `th()` in `src/stage/pixels.ts`, the matrix `src/art/battlebg.ts` already used.
- **A figure sorts as one unit.** A fighter is several objects (body, shadow, ring under the feet, health bar, a weapon smear while it strikes). If each gets its own layer they interleave wrongly: a far fighter's health bar draws over a near fighter's body. Instead every part takes the fighter's one depth number plus a fixed fraction (shadow -0.5, ring -0.4, body 0, smear +0.1, bar +0.25). The gap between two rows is 1000, so any part of a nearer fighter is above any part of a farther one. Unity calls this a Sorting Group. *Here:* `depthFor` and `PART` in `src/stage/config.ts`; the design's one known gap (bars drawn over everything) is gone.
- **The lunge borrows the target's row.** When an attacker steps up to a target, its feet row is the target's row, so a strict "lower on screen draws on top" tie would be decided by x alone. The design makes a lunging attacker sort by the target's row plus one, so its body overlaps the target's cleanly while they are in contact, and it goes back to its own row when it returns. *Here:* `sortRow` in `src/stage/config.ts`.
- **Depth haze baked into copies of the sprite.** Fading distant things toward a fog colour sells depth when nothing is scaled. A renderer's tint multiplies colours (darker, never toward a colour), works only on some renderers, and costs something every frame; blending toward fog is done once per sprite and row on its pixels, and the copy is kept as a texture (found again by name). *Here:* `hazedTexture` in `src/stage/textures.ts`, `depthTint` in the stage config (the acting hero and its target stay unhazed).
- **A HUD as data, drawn by small widgets.** Each region of a battle screen (turn timeline, party table, command icons, enemy box, banner, hit counter) has a box and a rule for when it shows in the stage config; the widget reads the box and draws its contents from a plain "view" (who is choosing, whose health is what). Moving a box is changing numbers, and the same view can be built from a test or a replay. *Here:* `src/stage/hud.ts`, `hudlayout.ts` (the pure rules), `demo.ts` (the view, built from the real battle engine).
- **A turn-order timeline (the ATB-style strip).** A line of chips across the top shows who acts next: the one acting now at the left (NOW), the rest in order along the line, heroes above it and enemies below so the two sides never mix up. The engine plans a whole round (everyone's action sorted by speed with a small random roll), so the strip is its queue; after the last of this round it shows the same people again as the next round. *Here:* `timelineLayout` in `src/stage/hudlayout.ts`, the queue from `Battle.roundOrder`.
- **Face crops from the sprites (and shrinking by exactly 2).** A HUD portrait is cut from the sprite already on the stage, so there is no second set of portraits to keep in step. Art painted at twice the screen resolution cannot be shrunk by a fraction without smearing pixels; cut it twice as big and shrink each 2x2 block to its commonest colour (the darker on a tie), and it stays crisp. *Here:* `cutFace` and `modeDown` in `src/stage/faces.ts`.
- **A game object count that stays flat (clean-up on every rebuild).** A panel that is rebuilt on every change is fine if each rebuild destroys what the last one made and removes the textures nobody shows any more. A test then counts: the same display-list length and texture count after cycling through every state twice proves nothing leaks. *Here:* `Hud.render` and `pruneTextures`, `e2e/stagelab-edit.spec.ts`.
- **A move as a frame list (data, not a script).** A fighting-game engine stores an attack as a list of frames: which picture, how many ticks it stays, a small offset, how far the body travels, and cues on a frame (`hit`, `glow`). Retiming a move is editing numbers, a tool can draw the list as a timeline, and a test can ask "what is on screen on tick 33?" without drawing anything. Rook's overhead strike is 14 such frames. *Here:* `src/data/moves.json`, `src/stage/moves.ts` (`compileMove`, `sampleMove`).
- **Startup, active, recovery (frame data).** Fighting-game words for a move's timing: startup is the ticks before the first hit lands, active the ticks the blows are landing, recovery what is left until the fighter is home. They are worked out from the frames and hit cues, never typed in. Rook's strike is 33 / 1 / 35. *Here:* `frameData` in `moves.ts`.
- **Reach as a fraction (lunges that fit any layout).** The distance to a target changes with the stage and the slot, so a lunge cannot be "move 70 pixels". Each frame says what share of the way to the target it covers (0.25 of the reach); the stage works out the full reach from where the target stands and how far the weapon reaches (`reachVector`). The shares add up to zero over a whole move, so the fighter comes home. *Here:* `move.dx`/`dz` in `moves.json`.
- **Facing-relative numbers.** A hero faces right and an enemy faces left, so a move's offsets say "forward" and "back" and the performer flips them for an enemy. One flinch serves both sides. *Here:* `offset` in `moves.json`, `dir` in `perform.ts`.
- **Two clocks: the world and the effects.** In a hitstop everything freezes for a few ticks, but the spark keeps swelling. So there are two counters: the world clock (moves, flinches, idle) stops, the effects clock (glow, shards, numbers, shake) does not. *Here:* `Performer.pause`, `StageScene.worldFrame`, `LiveFx.tick`.
- **Effects as arithmetic on age (instead of particles and tweens).** A particle emitter and a tween run on real milliseconds, so the same hit differs between a 60 Hz and a 144 Hz screen and cannot be stepped in a test. A shard's position at age n is a formula (a seeded direction, friction, gravity), rounded to whole pixels, so any tick can be asked directly and pixel art stays crisp. *Here:* `livefx.ts`.
- **Displayed state versus engine state.** The battle engine applies a whole action at once, but the player should see the health bar drop when the blade lands. The presentation keeps its own copy of everyone's health and status and advances it event by event, then checks it against the engine after each action so it can never drift. *Here:* `Disp` and `applyEvent` in `battleflow.ts`.
- **Waves of impacts (and combos that only look like three hits).** One blow landing on several targets at once is a wave; a second hit on the same target starts the next. When a move has more hit cues than the engine has waves (Kit's three blows, one damage roll) the waves take the LAST cues and the earlier blows are only seen. *Here:* `makeScript`, `Performer.start`.
- **Axis per picture (stills of different sizes).** Mark's Rook drawings are 53 to 110 px tall; instead of padding them to one size each keeps its own stand-on point, and the move draws it so that point lands on the fighter's ground spot. The point is found once: where the idle loop's own foot falls on the shared canvas. Measured front-boot error across all of Rook's frames: 0.5 px. *Here:* `strike.ts`, `stills.ts`.
- **A bounding box is not a body (contact from the silhouette).** The smallest rectangle around a figure sticks out wherever the figure does: a boss's shoulder pods, a sword's tip. A blow aimed at the rectangle's edge stops short of whatever is narrower at the height of the weapon (the legs). So a blow is aimed at the target's actual pixels on the row where the weapon is. Fighting games solve the same problem with hit and hurt boxes drawn per frame; here the pixels themselves are the hurtbox, because the art is already there. *Here:* `nearEdge` in `src/stage/contact.ts`; a `hit` event's `contact` in `src/data/moves.json`.
- **Swerve (running in front, not through).** In a diagonal party formation a straight run across the stage goes through the crewmates between the attacker and the target. Brawlers and side-view JRPGs avoid it by sending the runner along the nearest lane to the camera and letting it step back onto the target's row at the last moment, then home the same way. It is one more number per frame, a fraction of the distance down the stage to the front lane. *Here:* `move.sw` in the frame list and `clearLane` in `src/stage/contact.ts`.
- **Dash first, swing second (telegraph, travel, strike).** An attack that travels while it swings plays its smear across empty air, because the blade arrives after the blur. The reading order players expect is wind-up (the pose that says what is coming), travel (the body crosses the gap with the weapon already raised), then the swing that only has to cover the last short distance. *Here:* Rook's overhead frame is held through the dash and the four swing frames cover the last 0.41 of the distance.
- **Flash strength and the fading flash.** A hit flash that is fully white for the whole freeze reads as a cut-out silhouette. Fighting games step it down (white, then lighter, then the colour back) and flash a light hit less than a finisher; a red wash on the one who is hit says "hurt" where white says "struck". *Here:* `flash` and `tint` on a frame, `flash` on a hit event, and `Performer.freezeLook`.
- **A stand-in in-between (the kneel).** Until the artist draws a knocked-out pose, a quick stand-in can be made from the idle drawing by taking rows out of the legs and letting the upper body settle down by the same amount, with no scaling or rotating (either would smear pixel art). It is a placeholder named in the data (`$down`), replaced by naming a real still. *Here:* `src/stage/kneel.ts`.
- **Summons and phase changes are presentation events too.** The engine adds a fighter or changes a boss in one step, but the player should see the fighter arrive and the shell break. So the HUD keeps the old list and the old name until the stage draws the change (a summoned unit's engine id starts at 100, which is why the stage numbers fighters by their place in the list instead of by id). *Here:* `BattleFlow.hidden`, `reveal`, `showForm` and `slotOf`.
- **Retained mode versus immediate mode (why an editor wants an object list).** An immediate-mode renderer redraws the whole picture from the game's state every frame and keeps no list of what it drew: a fighter is a function call that works out its own position. A retained-mode one (Phaser's display list) keeps a list of objects, each with a position, a depth and a picture, and draws the list; the objects stay around between frames. A level editor wants the second kind, because "what is under the mouse" and "which comes first" are questions about a list, and in immediate mode the editor has to rebuild that list by recording each draw call. The catch is that the list is only worth as much as the rules on top of it: here the editor still picks fighters by their drawn pixels and the game still advances on its own fixed tick. *Here:* `BattleRenderer.render` (`src/scenes/battlekit/render.ts`) is immediate mode; `StageScene` (`src/stage/stagescene.ts`) keeps a `Fighter` record per figure and Phaser keeps the sprites.
- **Frame time: work versus interval, and percentiles (p50 and p95).** Two numbers describe how a game runs. The *interval* is the time between frames, which on a 60 Hz screen is 16.7 ms whatever the game does, because it waits for the screen. The *work* is how long the processor spent preparing the frame, which is the part the game controls and the part to keep under budget (here 6 ms). A *percentile* tells you about the bad frames an average hides: p50 (the median) is the frame in the middle, and p95 means 95 frames in 100 were at least this fast, so it shows the occasional hitch. A game can have a perfect interval and a growing work number (still fine until it nears the interval), or a high interval and tiny work (the screen is slow, not the game). *Here:* `FrameStats` in `src/stage/metrics.ts` records both, and the Battle Test's `timing:` test in `e2e/battletest.spec.ts` prints them.
- **Derive a counter, never store it (single source of truth).** A combo counter that keeps its own running totals can drift from the damage numbers beside it (one counts a hit the other missed, or a reset lands on a different frame). The fix is one list: every hit the party lands is a single entry, the floating number is drawn from that entry, and the counter prints the length and the sum of the list, so the two cannot disagree. A test then only has to add up the numbers it can see. *Here:* `src/stage/combo.ts` (`ShownHit`, `comboOf`), `Performer.comboList`, `ActView.hitList`.
- **Placing floating text by the target's bounds.** Damage numbers belong near the thing they hit but must not sit on its brightest part. The rule used by many games: above the head if there is room under the HUD, otherwise beside the sprite, and a second number takes the next free slot instead of overprinting the first. The sprite's own drawn bounds (not a fixed offset) say where the head is and how wide the body gets, so one rule serves a punk, a rat and a boss. A fill that contrasts with the target (here orange, against the white Warden) is the second line of defence. *Here:* `numberSpot` in `src/stage/hudlayout.ts`.

## Tooling and production

- **Battleground (stage) versus encounter (troop).** A *battleground* is a place where fights happen: a backdrop, a floor, the lanes to stand on and where each side stands. An *encounter* is who turns up to fight there. Keeping them apart is why one sewer can host rats on Monday and a boss on Friday, and why a setting on the battleground (how deep the shadows are) applies to every fight at that place, while a setting on the encounter (what the rats do) applies to that fight. RPG Maker calls them a battleback and a troop; a map names its battleground for each area. *Here:* a stage in `src/data/stages.json`, named by a map's `bg` (the Sinkline's fights use `sewer`); the troop editor is still to come (`docs/TOOLING-UI.md` 3.0 and 4.1), 2026-10-03.
- **One global HUD, a few per-stage overrides.** The battle menu (turn order, commands, party table) is the same in every fight, so it is one layout stored once, not a copy on every stage. A stage that truly needs a different box (a huge boss covers it) keeps just that box's changed numbers; everything else still follows the shared layout, so a later change to the shared layout reaches every stage that did not override it. *Here:* `src/data/hud.json` plus the optional `hud` on a stage, merged by `resolveStage` (`src/stage/config.ts`), 2026-10-03.
- **Axis lock (Shift while dragging).** Holding Shift turns a free drag into a straight one: whichever way you have moved further, sideways or up and down, is kept and the other is ignored. Design tools (Figma, Photoshop) all do it, so it feels natural; it is how you move something along a line without drifting. *Here:* every drag in the Battle Stage Editor (fighters, HUD boxes), 2026-10-03.
- **Align and distribute.** Align lines selected things up by an edge or a centre: with one thing selected it lines up with the page (or here, its half of the stage), with several it lines up with the others. Distribute spreads three or more with equal gaps. *Here:* the Align bar in the Battle Stage Editor; vertical align lands on depth rows, because a row is the only place to stand, 2026-10-03.
- **Live preview while a slider moves (input versus change).** A browser slider fires an `input` event on every tiny movement and a `change` event only when it is let go. An editor that listens only for `change` looks frozen while you drag. Listening for `input` updates the picture as you go; the undo step is still made on `change`, so one drag is one step. *Here:* every slider in the Battle Stage Editor, 2026-10-03.
- **Battleback (floor and wall).** RPG Maker draws a battle's background from two stacked pictures, a floor (battleback 1) and a wall or sky (battleback 2), which can be mixed freely so a few of each make many places ([MZ help](https://rpgmakerofficial.com/product/MZ_help-en/01_11_01.html)). *Here:* the Battle Stage Editor uses the same split plus a foreground framing layer drawn over the fighters (the street's rails) and an ambient layer such as rain (`docs/TOOLING-UI.md` §3.5), 2026-10-02.
- **Troop and encounter.** A troop is a named group of enemies that fight together, with where they stand and any scripted moments in the fight ("at half health, call for help"). An encounter list says which troops a map can throw at the player and how often, as a weight each ([MZ Troops](https://rpgmakerofficial.com/product/MZ_help-en/01_08_07.html)). *Here:* the stage decides *where* fighters may stand and the troop decides *who* stands there; the troop editor is planned (`docs/TOOLING-UI.md` §4.1).
- **Formation (slot sets by enemy count).** Pre-set places for one, two, three or four enemies, so every fight looks composed rather than squeezed. RPG Maker's Align button re-lays a troop evenly in entry order. *Here:* `enemies` in `src/data/stages.json`, keyed by count, with Align in the stage editor.
- **Battle test (playtest).** A button in an editor that starts a real fight with a party you choose (members, levels, equipment), so a design call is judged by playing it rather than imagining it. RPG Maker puts one on its Troops tab, and F9 during a playtest opens a debug screen ([MZ help](https://rpgmakerofficial.com/product/MZ_help-en/01_05.html)). *Here:* every Shadow Jog editor has a test button; the stage editor's runs on unsaved edits.
- **Database editor (list and form).** The standard shape for editing game data: a tab per kind (enemies, skills, items), a list of entries on the left, and a form for the selected entry on the right ([MZ Database](https://rpgmakerofficial.com/product/MZ_help-en/01_08.html)). *Here:* the layout every Shadow Jog tool shares (`docs/TOOLING-UI.md` §2.1).
- **Inspector and revert to default.** The properties panel of an editor, showing the fields of whatever is selected. Godot puts a revert arrow next to any value that differs from its default, so you can undo one change without undoing everything ([Godot inspector](https://docs.godotengine.org/en/stable/tutorials/editor/inspector_dock.html)). *Here:* the right-hand panel of every tool.
- **Template and override.** Define something once (a HUD layout, an enemy), use it many times, and let one use change a single value. That changed value is an override: it is shown differently (Tiled greys inherited values and shows overridden ones in black) and survives later edits to the template ([Tiled templates](https://doc.mapeditor.org/en/stable/manual/using-templates/)). *Here:* a stage's tweaks to the one global HUD layout (only the fields that differ are stored, `stages.json` › `hud`), and a troop's tweaks to a stage's formation.
- **WYSIWYG edit mode ("what you see is what you get").** Editing inside the real game scene rather than in a separate preview, so what you drag is exactly what the game draws. Separate previews drift from the game over time. *Here:* an exit criterion of the Phaser spike (`docs/spikes/phaser-stage.md`).
- **Undo stack and unsaved changes.** An editor records each change (one per gesture: a drag, a slider release) so it can be taken back and redone, and it marks the work as unsaved ("dirty") until saved, warning before the page closes. *Here:* the animation editor's 100-step stack becomes one shared module for every tool (`docs/TOOLING-UI.md` §2.5).
- **Gesture, snapshot undo and the dirty check.** An editor's undo step is a *gesture*, the whole drag or one slider release, never one mouse movement. The simplest reliable way to undo is to keep a snapshot of the whole data before each gesture and hand it back on Undo; it cannot drift out of step with the data the way a list of "what changed" can. "Unsaved changes" is then not a flag but a comparison of the data now with the data at the last save, so undoing back to the saved state clears it by itself. *Here:* `src/tools/undo.ts` and `src/stage/edit/session.ts` (the Battle Stage Editor).
- **Handles over the real scene.** A level editor shows the real game and draws its tools (lines, grips, badges, boxes) on a transparent layer on top, instead of drawing a copy. The layer is separate so nothing the editor draws can leak into the game, and its text stays sharp at any zoom. *Here:* the Battle Stage Editor's SVG overlay (`src/stage/edit/overlay.ts`) over the Phaser scene.
- **Draw order within a row (bring forward / send back).** When y-sorting puts two things at the same depth, the tie-break may overlap the wrong way round; a manual nudge (-1, 0, +1) fixes that pair without ever letting it outweigh a whole row, the way Tiled's manual draw order and design tools' bracket keys work. *Here:* `order` on a slot, Ctrl+] and Ctrl+[ in the stage editor (`depthFor` in `src/stage/config.ts`).
- **Scratch copy (a dry run for an editor's tests).** A way to run the real save and reload path without touching the real data file: the dev server reads and writes a private copy named in the page address. *Here:* `/stageedit.html?scratch=<name>`, used by `e2e/stageedit.spec.ts`.
- **AI art generation for games.** Tools like PixelLab make sprites, animations and tiles from text plus reference images. Results vary run to run, so the working method is: generate several options, review side by side at game scale, pick, then integrate. *Here:* the art pass and its review page (`docs/DEVELOPING.md` §8).
- **Store disclosure of AI content.** Storefronts such as Steam ask developers to disclose AI-generated content that players see (rules rewritten January 2026).
